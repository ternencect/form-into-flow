# Style and Review Guide

## Core visual relationships

Recognition anchors, transformed forms, continuous open space, and gestures establish the style together. These describe visual effects without requiring a particular reference collection, artist, movement, or physical process.

- Connect anchors to structure through boundaries, color layers, or linework. Their scale depends on the subject.
- Let major forms carry the gesture. Terrain, fabric, or shadows can become bending layers, dragged edges, and opaque planes that change spatial relationships.
- Alternate concentrated and extended passages. Place dark nodes and complex patches beside long dragged marks, supported by continuous quiet areas.
- Transition continuously between form and material. Contours can become fine lines, color layers, or arcs rather than separating the subject from an abstract background.
- Judge gradients, long curves, horizontal smears, and local texture by their contribution to the overall space.

Lighthouses, moons, trees, birds, and cliffs are optional motifs. Landscape relationships are not universal templates for people or animals.

## Apply the relationships

- **Open space is continuous low-detail space.** It may be bright, dark, or graded. Low saturation alone does not make an area quiet, and pale objects do not automatically count as negative space. Use no fixed empty-space ratio.
- **Structural lines organize color fields.** Dark nodes, long lines, and layers should emerge from source relationships. Networks or paint bands beside an intact subject may remain mere decoration.
- **Vary edges and opacity.** Place crisp short edges, dragged long edges, and lost edges beside one another. Alternate dense paint and thin color. Not every layer must be transparent or every contour broken.
- **Concentrate material without flattening its variety.** Mottling, pooled color, scratches, and fine cracks can gather at boundaries and nodes. Avoid a uniform coating across subject and background, but retain useful local complexity.
- **Retain color identity while changing area.** Extract characteristic colors and their value roles. Preserve night values or isolated highlights rather than imposing blue-pink, cream, or all-brown palettes.

## Subject constraints and possible approaches

Exploration areas are design options, not proven recipes. For people and animals, also consult [failure cases](failure-cases.md) to address conflicts between identity, anatomy, and spatial integration.

| Subject | Preserve | Explore | Avoid conflicting demands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Landscapes, buildings, streets | Landmarks, essential openings, characteristic light and color | Compress or extend ground planes, reflections, steps, or shadows | Keeping every scene detail while demanding broad open space |
| Close-up or half-body portraits | Face, hairstyle, pose, essential clothing, plausible hands | Transform clothing masses, shadows, or surrounding boundaries as part of the figure | Inventing unseen anatomy; making a face tiny while requiring close-up identity detail |
| Animals | Head features, distinctive markings, pose, plausible limbs | Mane, tail, ground contact, and surroundings | Adding only a base beneath an intact animal; paint resembling extra limbs; equating correct anatomy with detailed muscles |
| Plants | Characteristic flower shapes, counts, color markings | Branches, leaves, petal edges, and adjacent open space | Detailing every petal and leaf while demanding sparsity |
| Still life and products | Identity, counts, functional structures, specified text | Contours, shadows, surfaces, and contact points | Dissolving essential functions or merely adding ribbons beside an intact object |

For example, a golden mountain may retain its main silhouette and lit face while dark slopes or water are reorganized. Two persimmons may retain their count and branch direction while secondary foliage becomes lines and layers. These are optional approaches, not standard compositions.

## Five review criteria

| Criterion | Observable evidence | Common misjudgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural transformation — essential | A major source form is compressed, extended, dissolved, or converted into paint structure, changing spatial relationships | Treating blur, drips, cracks, or added ribbons as transformation |
| Density contrast — essential | Continuous quiet space adjoins concentrated detail and remains legible at thumbnail size | Equating low saturation, overall softness, or a smaller subject with density contrast |
| Recognition anchors | Essential identity, counts, structure, and pose remain legible at a scale suited to the constraints | Excusing identity or anatomy errors as abstraction |
| Material and structure | Fine lines, pooled color, and mottling follow structure; hard, soft, and lost edges are organized | Uniform fragments, canvas texture, or independent decorative linework |
| Color organization | Characteristic source colors remain traceable while area, value, and intensity establish focus | Applying a fixed palette or preserving each source region without reorganization |

Do not add up independent checkmarks to declare success. If the realistic subject and abstract background still read as separate layers, report the overall shortcoming. Improvements in individual criteria are only partial progress. See [evaluation limits](evidence-status.md) and [failure cases](failure-cases.md).
